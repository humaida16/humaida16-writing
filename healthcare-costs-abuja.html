The Price of Getting Sick in Abuja

What happens when healthcare costs more than a family can bear.

Picture a mother in Kubwa. Her child has a fever that won't break. She takes him to the local primary healthcare centre, pays for the consultation, then finds out the drugs aren't in stock.

So she walks to a private pharmacy and pays again, this time for more than she planned. By the end of the week, something else in the household budget has to give.

This isn't a rare story. It's the everyday reality for many families using primary healthcare centres across Abuja, and it's the story my research set out to understand.

Why this matters

Healthcare is supposed to be the first place people turn.

Primary healthcare should be the first point of contact when people need care. It should be accessible, affordable, nearby and reliable.

But across Abuja, the system is stretched. Facilities deal with underfunding, unpredictable government disbursements and a heavy reliance on payments made directly by patients.

To understand what this looks like for families, my research surveyed 208 households across the six area councils of the Federal Capital Territory: Abaji, AMAC, Bwari, Gwagwalada, Kuje and Kwali.

The numbers tell a hard story

The cost of care is not just measured in naira.

2–5 months of reported delays in facility disbursements
25–45% of facility needs covered by government allocations
₦5k–₦6k typical medication and testing costs
₦8k–₦12k reported maternal care costs

What families are paying

A consultation is only the beginning.

A consultation at a primary healthcare centre can cost between ₦1,000 and ₦1,500.

Then come the medicines, tests and other costs that follow. Medication and testing can add another ₦5,000 to ₦6,000.

Maternal care can reach ₦8,000 to ₦12,000.

For households living on an average monthly income of around $65, these amounts are not insignificant.

That’s not pocket change. It’s a choice between healthcare and everything else.

Insurance

Having insurance does not always mean feeling protected.

About 65% of respondents had some form of health insurance. Yet more than one-third strongly disagreed that insurance adequately protected them from high healthcare costs.

The research also found significantly higher rates of catastrophic health expenditure among married couples, people with secondary education, employed individuals and middle-income earners.

How families cope

When the money isn't there, something else has to give.

42.9% cut spending on essentials
18.6% delayed treatment
17.5% borrowed from family or friends
13.5% sold assets

It isn't all bad news

Some facilities are already finding ways to reduce the burden.

Community insurance schemes, tiered payment plans and partnerships with local NGOs are helping some families access care.

Facilities offering these options reported reductions in out-of-pocket costs of up to 35%.

Immunisation services were also reaching about half of children in the communities surveyed.

What needs to change

Five practical places to begin.

  1. Fix the disbursement pipeline. Facilities cannot plan properly when funding arrives months late.
  2. Make community-based health insurance more accessible. Premiums should reflect what households can realistically afford.
  3. Strengthen drug revolving funds. Bulk purchasing could help facilities keep essential medicines available.
  4. Offer flexible payment options. Families should not have to choose between treatment and basic household needs.
  5. Improve health-financing literacy. People need to understand what support exists and how they can access it.

The bigger picture

Healthcare financing is ultimately about people.

On paper, healthcare financing can look like another budget line.

In real life, it can mean whether a child receives treatment on time, whether a family goes into debt, or whether a mother has to choose between healthcare and everything else she needs to provide at home.

Abuja is the nation's capital. Its healthcare system should show what is possible when money reaches facilities on time and care is genuinely accessible and affordable.

Until then, the mother in Kubwa and thousands like her will continue to make impossible choices.

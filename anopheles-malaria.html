The Changing Dynamics of Anopheles Species

Impact of Person, Place and Time on the Epidemiology of Malaria in Africa
Written by Humaida Danjuma Khalid

Abstract

In the last two decades, efforts to control malaria by focusing on environmental and socioeconomic factors enhancing mosquito transmission have made significant progress but have not yet succeeded in stopping malaria in Africa. Malaria is a parasitic disease caused by Plasmodium protozoa and transmitted through the bites of infected Anopheles mosquitoes.

This paper examines the interaction of person, place and time factors that influence malaria transmission, with emphasis on how changes in vector distribution, behaviour and insecticide resistance interact with human and environmental variables in producing changes in transmission across diverse African ecological zones.

For the efficient development of malaria elimination programmes under rapidly changing epidemiological conditions, it is essential to understand the complex relationships between human behaviour, environmental factors and temporal patterns.

The findings highlight that vector–human interaction is dynamic and context-specific and calls for adaptation of strategies to reflect the changing nature of these interactions across Africa.

Keywords: Anopheles, insecticide resistance, malaria, temporal patterns, vector dynamics

1. Introduction

Malaria, caused by Plasmodium parasites, causes acute life-threatening infections and has a global public health impact. P. falciparum is widely distributed in Africa, while P. vivax, P. ovale and P. malariae have more limited geographical distributions.

The parasites are predominantly transmitted through the bites of infected Anopheles mosquitoes. Important malaria vectors in Africa include species within the Anopheles gambiae complex and Anopheles funestus group.

Malaria epidemiology varies geographically depending on local transmission intensity and endemicity. It is fundamentally shaped by interactions between Plasmodium parasites, human hosts, mosquito vectors and environmental factors.

Vector distribution, behaviour and characteristics can change in response to environmental variables such as temperature, rainfall and humidity, as well as anthropogenic factors including land-use transformation, urbanisation and vector-control interventions.

Understanding the changing relationships between person, place and time is therefore important for developing malaria surveillance and control strategies that can respond to evolving transmission patterns.

2. Objectives

  1. To analyse the intersection of person, place and time factors in malaria transmission dynamics.
  2. To determine surveillance systems capable of detecting and responding to changes in vector species composition, behaviour and insecticide resistance.
  3. To evaluate adaptive intervention strategies that address the changing dynamics of Anopheles mosquitoes.

3. Methods

This report presents findings from peer-reviewed literature published between 2018 and 2024. Literature was obtained from academic databases and search platforms including PubMed, African Journals Online and Google Scholar.

Keywords included Anopheles, malaria, Africa, vector dynamics, insecticide resistance, behaviour, climate change, urbanisation, vector-control interventions, human hosts and epidemiology.

Additional references were identified from relevant publications and reports from organisations including the World Health Organization, the Pan African Mosquito Control Association and National Malaria Control Programmes.

The dynamic relationship between vectors and malaria transmission was analysed using person, place and time factors. Person factors included demographics, behaviour and immunity. Place factors included geographical distribution and ecological conditions, while time factors included seasonal patterns and long-term trends.

4. Results

4.1 Person Factors

Person factors include genetic characteristics, acquired immunity, behaviour, demographics, culture and socioeconomic conditions. These characteristics can influence individual vulnerability and patterns of human–vector contact.

4.1.1 Human Demographics and Behaviours

Population growth, urbanisation and human mobility have shifted patterns of vector–human contact across Africa. Human mobility can contribute to malaria importation and connect areas with different transmission levels.

Migration resulting from environmental degradation, economic pressures, conflict and natural disasters may increase vulnerability in populations already living in high-risk settings. Cross-border mobility can also facilitate the movement of parasites and vectors with different resistance or behavioural characteristics.

4.1.2 Immune Patterns

Repeated exposure to malaria can lead to the development of partial clinical immunity, although this does not necessarily prevent reinfection. Asymptomatic infections may therefore remain an important component of transmission.

Children under five years of age have limited acquired immunity and remain particularly vulnerable. Pregnant women are also at increased risk because of biological and immunological factors associated with pregnancy.

Changes in population immunity can have important implications for malaria epidemiology, particularly in settings where successful control measures have reduced transmission.

4.1.3 Sleeping Habits and Outdoor Activities

Sleeping patterns and outdoor activities influence human–vector contact. When human activities overlap with periods of peak mosquito biting, exposure may occur before individuals are protected by insecticide-treated nets.

Outdoor evening activities may include household chores, security work, fishing and social or cultural activities. Seasonal changes in sleeping arrangements and outdoor behaviour can further produce different exposure profiles among population groups.

4.2 Place Factors

Place factors include natural environmental characteristics such as temperature, humidity, rainfall, soil quality, elevation and slope, as well as human-made changes including land-use transformation, deforestation, urbanisation, irrigation and infrastructure development.

4.2.1 Geographic Shifts in Vector Distribution

Environmental, climatic, seasonal and ecological factors influence the occurrence, transmission intensity and geographical distribution of malaria.

Temperature and precipitation influence mosquito survival, reproduction, biting activity and the development of malaria parasites within vectors. Changes in rainfall patterns can also alter the availability of suitable breeding habitats.

Human-made environmental changes can further modify transmission. Development projects, roads, dams, irrigation systems and agricultural practices may create or modify mosquito breeding habitats and alter human movement.

Urbanisation is particularly important because some malaria vectors can adapt to artificial environments and exploit breeding sites within densely populated areas.

4.2.2 Altitude

Altitude influences malaria transmission largely through its relationship with temperature and environmental conditions. Climate change may alter the suitability of higher-altitude environments for malaria vectors and parasites.

Such changes may expose populations with limited immunity to increased malaria risk and place additional pressure on health systems.

4.3 Time Factors

4.3.1 Seasonal Variations

Malaria transmission is strongly influenced by climatic conditions and has traditionally been associated with rainy seasons and warmer environments.

Changes in temperature and rainfall can alter mosquito development, parasite development and breeding-site availability. Unpredictable rainfall may result in unpredictable transmission patterns, while dry periods followed by heavy rainfall can create temporary breeding habitats and rapid increases in mosquito populations.

4.3.2 Long-Term Climate Trends

Climate change may influence malaria transmission through changes in mosquito development, reproduction, survival and geographical range.

Changing precipitation patterns can modify breeding habitats, while temperature changes may influence parasite development within mosquito vectors. These climate-related changes interact with human activities and environmental conditions to produce complex temporal patterns.

4.3.3 Vector Behaviour

Vector behaviour is an important consideration in malaria control. Insecticide-treated nets and indoor residual spraying primarily protect against mosquitoes that bite or rest in locations where they encounter these interventions.

When mosquitoes increasingly bite outdoors, bite earlier in the evening, or rest in locations where conventional interventions have limited effect, residual transmission may persist.

4.4 Intersection of Person, Place and Time

Malaria transmission is a dynamic process influenced by human behaviour, environmental conditions and mosquito biting patterns.

PERSON Human behaviour, immunity, demographics and mobility.
PLACE Environment, geography, urbanisation and breeding habitats.
TIME Seasons, climate trends and vector activity.

The interaction between these factors creates transmission landscapes that can differ substantially between communities and ecological zones.

Residual Transmission

Residual transmission refers to malaria transmission that continues despite implementation of standard vector-control interventions. Differences between mosquito biting behaviour and human protection can create opportunities for continued transmission.

Urban Malaria Transformation

Rapid urbanisation has created new person–place–time interactions. Human mobility can connect urban and rural transmission settings, while artificial breeding habitats may provide suitable environments for malaria vectors.

Highland Expansion

Changes in temperature, rainfall and land use may alter the suitability of highland environments for malaria transmission. Populations with limited previous exposure may be particularly vulnerable when transmission is introduced into these areas.

Insecticide Resistance Management Challenges

The widespread use of insecticide-based interventions has contributed to selection pressure for insecticide resistance in mosquito populations. Continued monitoring of resistance patterns is therefore important for maintaining the effectiveness of vector-control programmes.

5. Discussion

5.1 Implications for Surveillance

Effective malaria elimination requires surveillance systems capable of detecting changes in transmission, including changes that occur as the overall malaria burden declines.

Integrated surveillance combining entomological, parasitological, environmental and intervention-coverage data can provide a clearer picture of changing transmission dynamics.

Such systems should include:

  1. Regular identification of important vector species, including molecular approaches where necessary.
  2. Monitoring of mosquito biting and resting behaviour.
  3. Standardised approaches for detecting emerging insecticide-resistance mechanisms.
  4. Monitoring of climate and environmental variables that may influence vector populations.
  5. Better understanding of human behaviour and mobility patterns that influence exposure.

5.2 Implications for Intervention Strategies

Malaria control strategies have traditionally focused on reducing mosquito populations, preventing parasite transmission and increasing access to diagnosis and treatment.

However, changing vector populations and behaviours require increasingly adaptive approaches.

  1. Insecticide-resistance management: Resistance monitoring and appropriate management strategies are needed to maintain the effectiveness of insecticide-based interventions.
  2. Human-centred intervention design: Prevention strategies should reflect how people live, work, sleep and move within their communities.
  3. Climate-adaptive planning: Climate information can support planning around seasonal fluctuations and longer-term changes in malaria transmission.
  4. Cross-border and regional coordination: Collaboration across borders is important because human mobility and parasite transmission are not restricted by political boundaries.

5.3 Research Gaps

  1. Better characterisation of outdoor resting habitats and factors influencing outdoor biting behaviour.
  2. Improved understanding of insecticide-resistance mechanisms and their consequences for intervention effectiveness.
  3. Improved methods for measuring and modelling residual malaria transmission.
  4. Greater understanding of interactions between parasite and vector populations.
  5. Development and testing of new vector-control approaches for outdoor-biting and insecticide-resistant populations.
  6. Continued molecular research into malaria parasite biology, genetic diversity, treatment resistance and parasite–host interactions.

6. Conclusion

The changing dynamics of Anopheles species represent a significant challenge for malaria control in Africa.

Malaria transmission results from an intricate interaction between person, place and time. Human behaviour, environmental conditions, vector distribution, mosquito behaviour and insecticide resistance can all influence transmission patterns.

The person–place–time framework therefore provides a useful approach for understanding changing malaria transmission landscapes and designing responsive control strategies.

Future malaria-control efforts should move beyond one-size-fits-all approaches and consider local vector behaviour, environmental conditions, human mobility and patterns of exposure.

Integrating molecular technologies, climate information, entomological surveillance and human behavioural data may strengthen the ability of malaria programmes to identify emerging risks and respond appropriately.

Maintaining robust surveillance and adaptive intervention strategies will remain important even in settings where transmission has substantially declined, as changing epidemiological conditions may create opportunities for resurgence.

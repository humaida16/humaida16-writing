The Changing Dynamics of Anopheles Species

Impact of person, place and time on the epidemiology of malaria in Africa

Humaida Danjuma Khalid Literature Review · 2018–2024
01

Introduction

Malaria remains one of the most important infectious diseases affecting populations across Africa. Although transmission is strongly influenced by the presence of competent mosquito vectors, the process is far more complex than the simple presence or absence of Anopheles mosquitoes.

The epidemiology of malaria changes across populations, environments and periods of time. Human behaviour, immunity, housing, mobility, climate, land use, vector ecology and interventions can all influence where transmission occurs and how intensely it occurs.

“Vector-human interaction is dynamic and context specific.”

Understanding these changes requires attention to the interaction between person, place and time. These three dimensions provide a useful framework for understanding why malaria transmission does not remain constant across communities or throughout the year.

02

Background

Anopheles mosquitoes are the principal vectors responsible for human malaria transmission. More than 400 species of Anopheles have been described globally, although only a proportion are important malaria vectors.

Across Africa, vector populations differ in their geographical distribution, ecological preferences, biting behaviour and response to environmental conditions. These differences contribute to variation in malaria transmission between and within communities.

Changes in human settlement, agricultural practices, urbanisation, climate and vector-control programmes can further alter the relationship between humans and mosquito populations.

03

Objectives

01

To examine how person-related factors influence malaria transmission across African populations.

02

To explore how place and environmental conditions shape Anopheles distribution and malaria risk.

03

To understand how changes over time affect malaria transmission and vector dynamics.

04

Methodology

This literature review examined published evidence on Anopheles species and malaria transmission in Africa between 2018 and 2024.

Literature was considered in relation to human characteristics, environmental conditions, temporal changes, vector behaviour and malaria-control interventions.

Evidence from different African settings was considered to identify patterns and differences in malaria transmission across populations, geographical locations and periods of time.

Person,
Place,
Time

Three dimensions.
One changing transmission landscape.

People shape exposure

Human behaviour, immunity, occupation, age, socioeconomic conditions and mobility influence exposure to malaria vectors.

Individuals who spend more time outdoors at night, work in agricultural environments or live in poorly protected housing may experience different levels of exposure.

Environment shapes opportunity

Temperature, rainfall, vegetation, water availability and land use affect mosquito breeding and survival.

Environmental changes can therefore create or remove opportunities for vector populations to establish and expand.

Time shapes transmission

Malaria transmission changes over time. Seasonal rainfall, temperature patterns, population movement and changes in interventions can alter transmission intensity.

What is observed in one period may therefore differ substantially from what is observed months or years later.

06

Results

PERSON FACTORS

Malaria risk differs according to characteristics of the populations exposed to infection. Age, immunity, occupation, behaviour, socioeconomic status and mobility can all influence exposure and vulnerability.

Children and individuals with limited acquired immunity may experience greater risk of severe disease, while occupational exposure can increase contact with mosquitoes in particular settings.

PLACE FACTORS

Geography and environmental conditions influence where malaria vectors thrive. Rural agricultural communities, peri-urban settlements and rapidly expanding urban areas can present different ecological opportunities for transmission.

Changes in land use, drainage, housing and water management may alter mosquito breeding environments and consequently affect local transmission patterns.

TIME FACTORS

Malaria transmission is also temporal. Seasonal rainfall and temperature patterns can influence mosquito abundance, while population movement and intervention coverage can produce additional changes.

Long-term environmental and demographic changes may further influence the composition and behaviour of vector populations.

When person,
place and time meet

Malaria transmission is shaped by the interaction between people, environments and changing conditions over time.

A community may have suitable breeding environments, but the intensity of transmission will still depend on who lives there, how people interact with their environment and how these conditions change throughout the year.

Looking at these dimensions together therefore provides a more complete understanding of malaria epidemiology than examining any single factor in isolation.

08

Discussion

The evidence suggests that malaria transmission cannot be understood through vector presence alone. The relationship between humans and Anopheles mosquitoes changes according to social, environmental and temporal conditions.

This has important implications for malaria surveillance and intervention. Strategies that work effectively in one setting may require adaptation when ecological or behavioural conditions differ.

Understanding local transmission patterns is therefore essential for developing interventions that respond to the realities of individual communities.

09

Implications for Intervention

Malaria-control programmes need to account for the changing relationship between people, vectors and environments.

Surveillance should therefore extend beyond counting malaria cases to understanding where transmission is occurring, which populations are most exposed and how environmental and seasonal conditions are changing.

Combining epidemiological surveillance with entomological, environmental and behavioural information can support more responsive malaria-control strategies.

10

Research Gaps

Despite increasing research on malaria transmission, important gaps remain in understanding how rapidly changing environments influence vector populations across different African settings.

More locally specific studies are needed to examine changes in vector behaviour, species composition, human movement and environmental conditions over time.

Greater integration of entomological, epidemiological, environmental and social data may provide a clearer picture of changing transmission patterns.

11

Malaria transmission
is always moving

The changing dynamics of Anopheles species demonstrate that malaria transmission is not static. It is shaped by the continuous interaction between people, places and time.

Understanding these interactions is important for identifying populations at risk, anticipating changes in transmission and designing interventions that respond to local realities.

A closer understanding of person, place and time can therefore help move malaria control beyond a one-size-fits-all approach towards strategies that reflect the changing environments in which transmission occurs.

Understanding malaria means understanding the people, places and moments that allow transmission to happen.

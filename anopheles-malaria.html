The Changing Dynamics of
Anopheles Species

Impact of person, place and time on the epidemiology of malaria in Africa

Malaria • Anopheles • Public Health
Abstract

In the last two decades, efforts to control malaria by focusing on controlling environmental and socioeconomic factors enhancing mosquito transmission have made significant progress but they have not yet succeeded in stopping malaria in Africa; a parasitic disease caused by Plasmodium protozoa transmitted through Anopheles mosquito bites.

This paper looks at the interaction of person, place, and time factors that influence transmission of malaria, with emphasis on how changes in vector distribution, behavior, and insecticide resistance interact with human and environmental variables in producing change in transmission in diverse African ecological zones.

For the efficient development of a malaria elimination program under rapidly changing epidemiology, it is essential to understand the complicated linkages between human behavior, environmental factors, and temporal patterns.

Our findings highlight that “vector-human interaction is dynamic and context specific” and calls for adaptation of techniques that adopt the dynamic nature of interactions in Africa.

Keywords: Anopheles, insecticide resistance, malaria, temporal patterns, vector dynamics
Introduction

Malaria, caused by Plasmodium parasites, causes acute life-threatening infections and has a global health impact (Buck & Finnigan, 2023). P. falciparum is widely distributed in Africa and P. vivax, P. ovale, and P. malariae are rare and geographically restricted (Poespoprodjo et al., 2023).

The parasites are predominantly transmitted through the bites of infected Anopheles mosquitoes, notably An. gambiae sensu stricto, An. funestus, and An. arabiensis being most common in Africa (Poespoprodjo et al., 2023).

Malaria epidemiology varies geographically depending on the local malaria transmission intensity or endemicity class (Verma et al., 2025), but it is fundamentally shaped by Plasmodium parasites, human hosts, and environmental factors.

The incidence of malaria remains a major public health emergency across the world, with 247 million infections and 619,000 deaths projected for 2021 among 84 endemic countries (Lopes, 2025).

Only about 30 of the 537 Anopheles species worldwide (Lempang et al., 2023) are important malaria vectors (WHO, 2022).

Recent research shows that Anopheles species composition has shifted significantly across Africa, with the most efficient vectors belonging to the Anopheles gambiae sensu stricto and An. funestus groups (WHO, 2019).

Vector distribution, behavior and features have been significantly shifted due to environmental variables such as temperature, rainfall and humidity, as well as anthropogenic factors including land use transformation, urbanization and vector control actions (Wilke et al., 2021).

The growing social dynamic of malaria transmission across Africa needs an analysis of the person, place and time links that shape malaria transmission patterns.

In this research, an understanding of existing knowledge about evolving relationships is synthesized with implications for monitoring methods and control measures in an era with aggressive elimination aims.

Objectives
  1. To analyze the intersection of person, place, and time factors in malaria transmission dynamics
  2. To determine surveillance systems capable of detecting and responding to changes in vector species composition, behavior, and insecticide resistance
  3. To evaluate adaptive intervention strategies that address the changing dynamics of Anopheles mosquitoes
Methodology

This report presents findings from peer-reviewed literature published between 2018 and 2024, obtained from academic databases such as PubMed, African Journals Online, and Google Scholar.

Keywords used include “Anopheles”, “malaria”, “Africa”, “vector dynamics”, “insecticide resistance”, “behavior”, “climate change”, “urbanization”, “vector control interventions”, “human hosts”, and “epidemiology”.

Terms were used both individually and in combination to narrow searches inside titles, abstracts, keywords and specific journals.

More references were found from relevant publications and reports of the World Health Organization, Pan African Mosquito Control Association and National Malaria Control Programs.

If an empirical study presented data on environmental, demographic or intervention factors that resulted in changes in composition, behavior or ecology of African Anopheles species, then the study was considered.

The dynamics of vectors and malaria transmission were analyzed in terms of person factors, including population demographics, behavior and immunity; place factors, including geographic distribution and ecological conditions; and time factors, including seasonal patterns and long-term trends.

This provided a complete understanding of the evolving vector-human-environment connections in different African contexts using data from entomological surveillance, malaria case reporting systems, demographic surveys and intervention coverage assessments.

Results

Human demographics and behaviors

Population increases, urbanization, and mobility have shifted vector-human contact patterns across Africa (Saita et al., 2019).

Malaria importation has a considerable impact on transmission dynamics (Guerra et al., 2019; Iqbal et al., 2020).

Human migration connects places with varied transmission rates, allowing disease to persist even in low-transmission situations (Porter et al., 2020).

Traditional vectors, such as An. gambiae sensu stricto, are being replaced in urban areas by adaptable An. arabiensis and An. coluzzii, which flourish in contaminated breeding grounds and exhibit a variety of biting behaviours.

Malaria vulnerability rises because of conflict and displacement. Migration owing to environmental degradation, economic need and natural calamities has a negative impact on the poor in hazardous locations.

Cross-border mobility promotes the spread of parasites and vectors that have resistance mechanisms or behavioural features (Martens & Hall, 2020).

Immune patterns

After repeated exposure, some clinical immunity develops but does not protect against reinfection, resulting in asymptomatic patients (Bansal & Kumar, 2024).

Increased immunity reduces vulnerability and infectivity. Shifting immunity profiles have a substantial impact on malaria epidemiology, especially in regions where control measures have diminished transmission.

The immunity of pregnant women is weak, which makes them more vulnerable to exposure near places where vector breeding takes place. Hence, for biological and social reasons, women, mainly pregnant women, and children are most at risk of malaria in both high and low malaria endemic regions.

A Ugandan study found that females were twice as likely to have a malaria case compared to males, and the largest incidence was within females older than 39 years (Okiring et al., 2022).

Children under the age of five lack immunity, highlighting the need for age structuring. The intricate interplay between immunity levels and asymptomatic parasitemia continues to impede elimination attempts, with human reservoirs supporting transmission even as the illness burden declines.

Sleeping habits and outdoor activities

Sleeping and outdoor activities have a substantial impact on human-vector contact patterns across Africa (Monroe et al., 2019).

The overlap between sleeping hours and peak Anopheles biting allows for critical window exposure. An. gambiae sensu stricto, An. funestus, and An. coluzzii are usually anthropophilic, feeding and resting indoors.

In rural Africa, traditional early sleeping practices, characterized by early retirement and early waking, were once associated with decreased vector activity, but modernity has changed to longer bedtimes, particularly in urban regions.

Outdoor evening activities, including home chores, security work, fishing and cultural events, have an impact on time spent outdoors and ITN use (Monroe et al., 2019), increasing exposure to Anopheles mosquitoes when preventive measures are ineffective (Shah et al., 2019).

In East Africa, 40% of contagious bites took place when people were awake but not under bed nets. LLIN use frequently fails to correspond with actual exposure risk, with nets being placed after 10 p.m., even though considerable biting can occur between 6 and 10 p.m.

Sleeping arrangements such as bed-sharing practices, sleeping or outdoor location selections during hot seasons, and seasonal fluctuation in sleeping patterns and outdoor activities generate diverse risk profiles within groups.

Geographic shifts in vector distribution

Environmental, climatic, seasonal and ecological factors determine the occurrence, transmission intensity and geographical distribution of malaria (Kibret et al., 2019).

Natural environment

Precipitation and air temperature are climate variables that have a significant impact on the spatial distribution and relative abundance of malaria vector species in Africa.

Climate can affect geographic range and malaria distribution and the subsequent development of malaria, which directly affects survival, reproduction and biting rates and increases infectivity (Mordecai et al., 2020).

Where heavy rainfall is predominant, An. gambiae sensu stricto is paramount, while An. arabiensis is more frequent in desert-style areas (Kibret et al., 2019).

Their geographical distribution has also started to shift significantly across Africa. Historical dominance patterns are changing, with An. arabiensis expanding its range in East Africa at the expense of An. gambiae sensu stricto, likely due to the former's greater tolerance for drier conditions and zoophilic feeding habits that help it evade indoor interventions.

The frequency, duration and intensity of precipitation contribute to the formation of suitable water pools for mosquito breeding.

Man-made environment

The implementation of development projects such as roads, railways, dams and irrigation systems often results in social and environmental impacts (Andrade et al., 2022).

Such consequences can generate excellent breeding grounds for mosquitoes, movement of naïve populations to malaria-endemic areas, or migration of infected persons to locations where malaria vector is prevalent and transmission is suitable.

Agriculture's impact on malaria is determined by crop type, planting strategy, irrigation practice and Anopheles characteristics.

Rice fields can be good breeding grounds for mosquitoes if irrigation is not properly managed.

The An. funestus group, which was formerly associated with more stable water bodies, has risen in places where agricultural irrigation has generated favourable breeding habitats.

The availability of basic infrastructure in residential areas might also influence malaria infection exposure, depending on the features of local malaria vectors.

An. coluzzii has shown extraordinary adaptability to urban contexts in West Africa, reproducing in permanent man-made containers and polluted water bodies that were previously thought to be inappropriate for Anopheles mosquitoes.

This modification has allowed year-round transmission in metropolitan areas that formerly exclusively experienced seasonal malaria.

Altitude

Climate change will increase exposure to vector-borne disease at higher latitudes and altitudes. Additionally, the global population of malarial mosquitoes increases the risk of locally transmitted malaria in non-endemic regions adjacent to endemic areas, especially at higher altitudes (Kreppel et al., 2019).

Malaria infections may rise at greater elevations due to climate change, as well as altitudinal shifts in suitable habitat.

Mosquitoes are generally found at low elevations and on low slopes (Kim et al., 2018). Monthly malaria case incidence varies with altitude, and monthly rainfall has a beneficial effect on malaria risk (Hasyim et al., 2018).

Seasonal variations

Climate change is changing patterns of malaria transmission throughout Africa.

Malaria has always been seen as a climate-sensitive illness, with transmission traditionally connected with summer months in temperate zones and wet lowlands in tropical countries (Nissan et al., 2021).

In recent years, statistical and dynamical transmission models driven by climate variables have been used to anticipate likely changes in the geographical distribution of disease in a warmer world.

According to some climate-based models, malaria transmission and burden could rise in temperate zones while contracting in other places (Altizer et al., 2018).

Nevertheless, malaria incidence ratios are reduced by excessive daytime temperatures (Diouf et al., 2020), whereas hosts in places with marked rainfall seasonality typically have higher host specificity (Fecchio et al., 2019).

Incubation times of Plasmodium are shortened at warmer temperatures, a phenomenon that allows transmission to be accelerated (Thomas et al., 2018).

Studies indicate that rainfall unpredictability leads to unpredictable patterns. Dry spells followed by heavy rains promote explosive vector proliferation and difficult-to-control transmission surges.

Extreme weather events such as heatwaves, flooding and droughts caused by climate change exacerbate this dynamic (Akhtar, 2020; Sam et al., 2020).

Long-term climate trends

Climate change can cause heatwaves, flooding, heavy rains, droughts and windstorms, among other extreme weather events (Akhtar, 2020; Sam et al., 2020).

Research has shown that climate change provides for an increase in vector-borne diseases. Larval growth is sped up by higher temperatures, reproduction is improved, blood-feed frequency is increased, lifecycles of vectors are shortened and mortality rates decreased, leading to more frequent and severe epidemics.

Rainfall increases cases, provides aquatic stages that prolong mosquito lifespans and broadens the vector range, while droughts can expand vector range as new breeding sites form (Megersa & Luo, 2025).

These climate-related changes interact with anthropogenic causes, resulting in complicated temporal patterns that hamper forecasting and control attempts.

Vector behavior

Bed net coverage in Africa has led to changes in the behavior of biting Anopheles mosquitoes, with an increasing proportion of bites happening outside and while humans are not protected by bed nets.

Indoor-based therapies are rendered less effective due to this behavioral adaptation, allowing residual malaria transmission to persist.

Outdoor resting sites for Anopheles mosquitoes have resulted in alternative breeding and resting patterns, continuing to contribute to transmission in the absence of protective clothing and repellents, particularly when bed nets provide limited protection (Amimo, 2023).

When person, place and time meet

Malaria transmission changes when people, environments and mosquito behavior intersect

The connections between person, place and temporal factors result in transmission landscapes that differ greatly across Africa. These intersections are particularly visible in four important phenomena.

01

Residual transmission

Residual transmission is parasite transmission that continues after completely operational net coverage.

Despite the success of ITNs, their effectiveness in sub-Saharan Africa varies greatly, with several countries experiencing high illness incidence despite high bed net penetration (Topazian et al., 2021).

Persistent transmission occurs through interactions between human behaviors, location characteristics and temporal changes in vector behavior.

The extent of residual malaria transmission varies substantially among epidemiological contexts, depending on local entomological and anthropological factors (Guglielmo et al., 2021).

02

Urban malaria transformation

Rapid urbanization in Africa has resulted in novel person-place- time interactions, with adapted Anopheles populations exploiting permanent breeding sites in densely populated areas.

Human mobility patterns can facilitate parasite movement between urban and rural zones, while artificial breeding habitats can enable year-round transmission (Kabaria et al., 2017).

This illustrates the intricate and adaptive nature of malaria transmission systems in which humans, vectors, the environment and parasites interact in an iterative and nonlinear manner (Savi et al., 2021).

03

Highland expansion

Malaria transmission is strongly linked to height and rainfall (Lemma, 2021).

Changes in malaria-introducing locations and intensities, warming temperatures, agricultural development and changing immune profiles have contributed to the introduction of malaria into malaria-free highland regions.

Such expansion produces more vulnerable groups and can place additional strain on healthcare systems that are inadequate to support malaria control (Siraj et al., 2021).

04

Insecticide resistance management challenges

The use of ITNs and indoor residual spraying to suppress malaria vectors, coupled with case management, has contributed to the expansion of insecticide-resistant vectors in many endemic countries (WHO, 2021).

Rapid spread of insecticide resistance now encompasses most sub-Saharan African malaria-endemic countries and may threaten the success of vector control (Suh et al., 2023).

Resistance spread depends on the location where it occurs and the tactics used for human intervention deployment.

This reinforces the need for locally tailored resistance management rather than one-size-fits-all solutions.

Discussion

Implications for surveillance

A monitoring system with high sensitivity and specificity to detect the presence or absence of malaria infections without ambiguity should be used to assess malaria elimination locally.

This means that the system should be able to correctly detect rare or asymptomatic cases while excluding other illnesses from being misclassified as malaria (Nelli et al., 2024).

Efficient malaria monitoring and control requires understanding how people, places and time interact with one another.

Better surveillance systems are needed that can detect and respond to changes in vector species composition and behavior.

Integrated surveillance that combines entomological, parasitological and intervention coverage data at source, site and subdistrict levels gives a clearer picture of ongoing changes in transmission dynamics.

Existing species complexes cannot be separated by physical identification alone due to varying behavioral features and vectorial capacity. Regular identification at the molecular level is therefore important.

Behavioral surveillance should employ a variety of methods to collect biting and resting behaviors, including barrier screens and host decoy traps.

Resistance monitoring should use standardized approaches to detect emerging resistance mechanisms in real time.

Climate and environmental monitoring can help predict vector fluctuations in response to ecological variables.

Human behavior and mobility data can improve understanding of how changing vector behaviors increase or decrease transmission opportunities.

To detect resurgence risks associated with changing vector dynamics or waning intervention effectiveness, enhanced surveillance approaches must be maintained even as the malaria burden decreases.

Implications for intervention strategies

Most intervention strategies have concentrated on reducing Anopheles mosquito populations through LLINs and IRS, blocking Plasmodium transmission, and increasing access to diagnostics and treatment (Amimo, 2023).

These interventions are proving effective, but hurdles and constraints remain. The shifting dynamics of Anopheles populations require more diverse and adaptive intervention options.

Insecticide resistance management

Integrated vector management, insecticide rotation, PBO nets, long-lasting larvicides and non-pyrethroid insecticides for IRS have been proposed as part of adaptive approaches (Zhou et al., 2020).

Human-centered intervention design

Human-centered design supports malaria prevention programs in matching a family's convenience, comfort and lifestyle to encourage sustained access and use (Macdonald & Putzer, 2019).

Climate-adaptive planning

Understanding changing climate patterns can support flexible planning around seasonal fluctuations and long-term changes in intervention timing and intensity (Nissan et al., 2021).

Cross-border and regional coordination

Coordinated anti-malaria activities can reduce the risk of parasites or resistant vector populations being reintroduced across political boundaries.

Research gaps

Characterization of outdoor resting habitats and the ecological and behavioral factors determining outdoor biting.

Clarification of the interplay between metabolic, target-site and other resistance mechanisms and their operational consequences for intervention efficacy.

Improved tools for measuring and modeling residual transmission.

Evaluation of interactions between parasites and vectors and the selection of more virulent or transmissible parasite strains.

Development and testing of novel vector control tactics across diverse ecological settings, with a focus on outdoor-biting and insecticide-resistant populations.

Molecular biology research aimed at understanding the mechanisms underpinning the biology of Plasmodium, including its life cycle, genetic diversity, treatment resistance mechanisms and interactions with humans.

Conclusion

Understanding malaria through person, place and time

Anopheles species dynamics in Africa represent a challenge and an opportunity in the context of malaria control initiatives, but the former are currently dominant.

Malaria transmission is the result of an intricate interaction between the person, place and time and therefore integrated surveillance, research and intervention design techniques are needed.

Since vector populations have mastered the skill of adapting to changing environments and changing control measures, surveillance systems must keep pace with evolving vector transmission dynamics.

Similarly, intervention efforts must shift beyond one-size-fits-all approaches to accommodate the unique problems presented by evolving vector behaviours, pesticide resistance and human movement patterns.

With the incorporation of molecular technologies, climate data and studies of human behavior, malaria research can better understand the biological and ecological processes that drive transmission.

Continuing worldwide efforts to eradicate malaria will require maintaining robust surveillance and applying adaptive control in areas where transmission seems to have declined, which may otherwise result in its return.

Using the person-place-time framework offers a powerful tool to understand malaria transmission dynamics and develop responsive control plans tailored to changing epidemiological situations.

The Changing Dynamics
of Anopheles Species

How person, place and time shape the epidemiology of malaria across Africa.

Humaida Danjuma Khalid Literature Review · 2018–2024
01

Malaria transmission is not static. The mosquitoes that transmit it, the environments they inhabit, and the people they encounter are constantly changing.

In the last two decades, efforts to control malaria by focusing on environmental and socioeconomic factors that enhance mosquito transmission have made significant progress. However, these efforts have not yet succeeded in stopping malaria in Africa.

This research examines the interaction between person, place and time and how these factors influence malaria transmission across diverse African ecological zones.

ABSTRACT

In the last two decades, efforts to control malaria by focusing on controlling environmental and socioeconomic factors enhancing mosquito transmission have made significant progress but they have not yet succeeded in stopping malaria in Africa; a parasitic disease caused by Plasmodium protozoa transmitted through Anopheles mosquito bites.

This paper looks at the interaction of person, place, and time factors that influence transmission of malaria, with emphasis on how changes in vector distribution, behavior, and insecticide resistance interact with human and environmental variables in producing change in transmission in diverse African ecological zones.

For the efficient development of a malaria elimination program under rapidly changing epidemiology, it is essential to understand the complicated linkages between human behavior, environmental factors, and temporal patterns.

Our findings highlight that vector-human interaction is dynamic and context specific and calls for adaptation of techniques that adopt dynamic nature of interactions in Africa.

Keywords: Anopheles · insecticide resistance · malaria · temporal patterns · vector dynamics
02

Introduction

Malaria, caused by Plasmodium parasites, causes acute life-threatening infections and has a global health impact (Buck et al., 2023). P. falciparum is widely distributed in Africa and P. vivax, P. ovale, and P. malariae are rare and geographically restricted (Poespoprodjo et al., 2023).

The parasites are predominantly transmitted through the bites of infected Anopheles mosquitoes, notably An. gambiae sensu stricto, An. funestus, and An. arabiensis being most common in Africa (Poespoprodjo et al., 2023).

Malaria epidemiology varies geographically depending on the local malaria transmission intensity or endemicity class (Verma et al., 2025), but it is fundamentally shaped by Plasmodium parasites, human hosts, and environmental factors.

The incidence of malaria remains a major public health emergency across the world, with 247 million infections and 619,000 deaths projected for 2021 among 84 endemic countries (Lopes, 2025).

“ Vector-human interaction is dynamic and context specific.”

Only about 30 of the 537 Anopheles species worldwide (Lempang et al., 2023) are important malaria vectors (WHO, 2022). Recent research shows that Anopheles species composition has shifted significantly across Africa, with the most efficient vectors belonging to the Anopheles gambiae sensu stricto and An. funestus groups (WHO, 2019).

Vector distribution, behavior and features have been significantly shifted due to environmental variables such as temperature, rainfall and humidity, alongside anthropogenic factors including land use transformation, urbanization and vector control actions (Wilke et al., 2021).

The growing social dynamic of malaria transmission across Africa needs an analysis of the person–place–time links that shape malaria transmission patterns. In this research, an understanding of existing knowledge about evolving relationships is synthesized with implications for monitoring methods and control measures in an era with aggressive elimination aims.

Objectives

01

04

Methodology

This report presents findings from peer-reviewed literature published between 2018 and 2024, obtained from academic databases such as PubMed, African Journals Online, and Google Scholar.

Keywords used include Anopheles, malaria, Africa, vector dynamics, insecticide resistance, behavior, climate change, urbanization, vector control interventions, human hosts, and epidemiology. Terms were used both individually and in combination to narrow search inside titles, abstracts, keywords and specific journals.

More references were found from relevant publications and reports of the World Health Organization, Pan African Mosquito Control Association and National Malaria Control Programs.

If an empirical study presented data on environmental, demographic or intervention factors that resulted in changes in composition, behavior or ecology of African Anopheles species, then the study was considered.

The dynamic vector and malaria transmission is analyzed in terms of person factors (people demography, behavior, immunity), place factors (geographic distribution, ecological conditions), and time factors (seasonal patterns, long-term trend).

This provided a complete knowledge of the evolving vector-human- environment connections in different African contexts using data from entomological surveillances, malaria case reporting systems, demographic surveys and intervention coverage assessments.

06

Results

4.1 PERSON FACTORS

The person factor includes genetic, acquired immunity, behavior, demography, culture, socioeconomic features and so on.

Human Demographics and Behaviors

Population increases, urbanization, and mobility have shifted vector-human contact patterns across Africa (Saita et al., 2019). Malaria importation has a considerable impact on transmission dynamics (Guerra et al., 2019; Iqbal et al., 2020).

Human migration connects places with varied transmission rates, allowing disease to persist even in low-transmission situations (Porter et al., 2020).

Traditional vectors, such as An. gambiae sensu stricto, are being replaced in urban areas by adaptable An. arabiensis and An. coluzzii, which flourish in contaminated breeding grounds and exhibit a variety of biting behaviours.

Malaria vulnerability rises because of conflict and displacement. Migration owing to environmental degradation, economic need, and natural calamities has a negative impact on the poor in hazardous locations.

Cross-border mobility promotes the spread of parasites and vectors that have resistance mechanisms or behavioural features (Martens & Hall, 2020).

Immune Patterns

After repeated exposure, some clinical immunity develops but does not protect against reinfection, resulting in asymptomatic patients (Bansal & Kumar, 2024).

Increased immunity reduces vulnerability and infectivity. Shifting immunity profiles have a substantial impact on malaria epidemiology, especially in regions where control measures have diminished transmission, such as East African highlands.

The immunity of pregnant women is weak which makes them more vulnerable to families that come near the places where vector breeding takes place. Hence, for biological and social reasons, women, mainly pregnant women, and children are most at risk of malaria in both high and low malaria endemic regions.

A Ugandan study found that females were twice as likely to have a malarial case compared to males, and the largest incidence was within females older than 39 years (Okiring et al., 2022).

Children under the age of five lack immunity, highlighting the need of age structuring. The intricate interplay between immunity levels and asymptomatic parasitemia continues to impede elimination attempts, with human reservoirs supporting transmission even as the illness burden declines.

Sleeping Habits and Outdoor Activities

Sleeping and outdoor activities have a substantial impact on human-vector contact patterns across Africa (Monroe et al., 2019). The overlap between sleeping hours and peak Anopheles biting allows for critical window exposure.

An. gambiae sensu stricto, An. funestus, and An. coluzzii are usually anthropogenic, feeding and resting indoors.

Outdoor evening activities such as home chores, security work, fishing and cultural events have had an impact on time spent outdoors and ITN use (Monroe et al., 2019) due to increased exposure to Anopheles mosquitoes when preventive measures are ineffective (Shah et al., 2019).

40% of contagious bites in parts of East Africa took place when people were awake but not under bed nets.

In East Africa, including Tanzania, Kenya and Uganda, LLIN use frequently fails to correspond with actual exposure risk, with nets being placed after 10p.m., even though considerable biting can occur between 6 and 10p.m.

Sleeping arrangements such as bed sharing practices, sleeping/outdoor location selections during hot seasons, and seasonal fluctuation in sleeping patterns and outdoor activities generate diverse risk profiles within groups.

4.2 PLACE FACTORS

These variables are influenced by both the natural environment and the man-made environment.

Geographic Shifts in Vector Distribution

Environmental, climatic, seasonal and ecological factors determine the occurrence, transmission intensity and geographical distribution of malaria (Kibret et al., 2019).

Natural Environment

Precipitation and air temperature have a significant impact on the spatial distribution and relative abundance of malaria vector species in Africa. Climate can affect geographic range and malaria distribution and subsequently affect malaria survival, reproduction and biting rates (Mordecai et al., 2020).

Where heavy rainfall is predominant, An. gambiae sensu stricto is paramount, while An. arabiensis is more frequent in desert-style areas (Kibret et al., 2019).

Their geographical distribution has also started to shift significantly across Africa. Historical dominance patterns are shifting, with An. arabiensis expanding its range in East Africa at the expense of An. gambiae sensu stricto, likely due to the former's greater tolerance for drier conditions and zoophilic feeding habits that help it evade indoor interventions.

Man-made Environment

Development projects such as roads, railways, dams and irrigation often result in social and environmental impacts (Andrade et al., 2022).

Such consequences can generate excellent breeding grounds for mosquitoes, movement of naïve populations to malaria-endemic areas, or migration of infected persons to locations where malaria vector is prevalent and transmission is suitable.

Agriculture's impact on malaria is determined by crop type, planting strategy, irrigation practice, and Anopheles characteristics. Rice fields can be good breeding grounds for mosquitoes if irrigation is not properly managed.

The availability of basic infrastructure in residential areas might also influence malaria infection exposure, depending on the features of the local malaria vectors.

An. coluzzii has shown extraordinary adaptability to urban contexts in West Africa, reproducing in permanent manmade containers and polluted water bodies that were previously thought to be inappropriate for Anopheles mosquitoes.

Altitude

Climate change will increase exposure to vector-borne disease at higher latitudes and altitudes. The global population of malarial mosquitoes increases the risk of locally transmitted malaria in non-endemic regions adjacent to endemic areas, especially at higher altitudes (Kreppel et al., 2019).

Malaria infections may rise at greater elevations due to climate change, as well as altitudinal shifts in suitable habitat.

4.3 TIME FACTORS

Seasonal Variations

Climate change is changing patterns of malaria transmission throughout Africa. Malaria has traditionally been connected with summer months in temperate zones and wet lowlands in tropical countries (Nissan et al., 2021).

In recent years, a range of statistical and dynamical transmission models of malaria transmission, driven by climate variables, have been used to anticipate likely changes in the geographical distribution of disease in a warmer world.

Incubation times of Plasmodium are shortened at warmer temperatures, a phenomenon that allows transmission to be accelerated (Thomas et al., 2018).

Studies indicate that rainfall unpredictability leads to unpredictable patterns. Dry spells followed by heavy rains promote explosive vector proliferation and difficult-to-control transmission surges.

Long-term Climate Trends

Climate change can cause heatwaves, flooding, heavy rains, droughts and windstorms, among other extreme weather events (Akhtar, 2020; Sam et al., 2020).

Research has shown that climate change provides for an increase in vector-borne diseases. Larval growth is sped up by higher temperatures, reproduction is improved, blood feed frequency is increased, lifecycles of vectors are shortened and mortality rates decreased, leading to more frequent and severe epidemics.

These climate-related changes interact with anthropogenic causes, resulting in complicated temporal patterns that hamper forecast and control attempts.

Vector Behavior

Bed net coverage in Africa has led to changes in the behavior of biting Anopheles mosquitoes, with an increasing proportion of bites happening outside and while humans are not protected by bed nets.

Indoor-based therapies are rendered less effective by this behavioral adaptation, allowing residual malaria transmission to persist. Outdoor resting sites for Anopheles mosquitoes have resulted in alternative breeding and resting sites, a pattern that continues to contribute to transmission in the absence of protective clothing and repellents (Amimo, 2023).

When person,
place and time meet

Human activity, environmental conditions and mosquito biting patterns intersect to shape malaria transmission.

01

Residual transmission

Parasite transmission can continue after operational net coverage because human behaviour, outdoor activity, vector behaviour and timing can create exposure that standard interventions do not fully address.

02

Urban malaria transformation

Rapid urbanization has created novel person-place-time interactions, with adapted Anopheles populations exploiting permanent breeding sites and human mobility facilitating parasite movement.

03

Highland expansion

Warming temperatures, agricultural development and changing immunity profiles can contribute to the introduction of malaria into highland regions where transmission was previously limited.

04

Insecticide resistance

The spread of insecticide resistance presents an evolving challenge for vector control. Resistance patterns vary according to location, vector populations and intervention strategies.

08

Discussion

5.1

Implications for Surveillance

A monitoring system with high sensitivity and specificity to detect the presence or absence of malaria infections without ambiguity should be used to assess malaria elimination locally.

Efficient malaria monitoring and control requires understanding how people, places, and time interact with one another. Better surveillance systems are needed that can detect and respond to changes in vector species composition and behavior.

Integrated surveillance that combines entomological, parasitological and intervention coverage data at the source, site and subdistrict levels gives a clearer picture of ongoing changes in transmission dynamics.

01

Regular identification at the molecular level where species complexes cannot be separated by physical identification alone.

02

Employing a variety of methods to collect biting and resting behaviours of different sampling groups.

03

Standardized approaches to detect emerging resistance mechanisms.

04

Monitoring climate and environment to better predict vector fluctuations.

05

Better understanding of how human behaviour and mobility influence transmission risk.

To detect resurgence risks associated with changing vector dynamics or waning intervention effectiveness, enhanced surveillance approaches must be maintained even as the malaria burden decreases.

5.2

Implications for Intervention Strategies

Most intervention strategies have concentrated on reducing Anopheles mosquito populations through LLINs and IRSs; blocking Plasmodium transmission; and increasing access to diagnostics and treatment (Amimo, 2023).

These interventions are proving effective, but there remain hurdles and constraints that restrict their ability to reduce malaria transmission.

01

Insecticide resistance management

Integrated vector management, insecticide rotation, PBO nets, larvicides and non-pyrethroid insecticides are among adaptive approaches being explored.

02

Human-centered intervention design

Prevention programs need to account for family convenience, comfort, lifestyle and the activities that overlap with peak vector biting periods.

03

Climate-adaptive planning

Changing climate patterns need to be incorporated into seasonal planning, intervention timing and intensity.

04

Cross-border coordination

Regional coordination can help limit the movement and reintroduction of parasites and resistant vector populations.

5.3

Research Gaps

01

Characterization of outdoor resting habitats and the ecological and behavioural factors determining outdoor biting.

02

Clarification of the interplay between resistance mechanisms and their operational consequences for intervention efficacy.

03

Improved tools for measuring and modelling residual transmission.

04

Evaluation of interactions between parasites and vectors, including selection of more virulent or transmissible strains.

05

Development and testing of novel vector control tactics in diverse ecological settings.

06

Molecular biology research into the mechanisms underpinning the biology of Plasmodium species.

09
CONCLUSION

Malaria transmission
is always moving.

Anopheles species dynamics in Africa represent a challenge and an opportunity in the context of malaria control initiatives, but the former are currently dominant.

Malaria transmission is the result of an intricate interaction between the person, place and time and therefore integrated surveillance, research and intervention design techniques are needed.

Since vector populations have mastered the skill of adapting to the changing environment and changing effective control measures, surveillance systems must keep pace with evolving vector transmission dynamics.

Similarly, intervention efforts must shift beyond one-size-fits-all approaches to accommodate the unique problems given by evolving vector behaviours, pesticide resistance, and human movement patterns.

With the incorporation of molecular technologies and climate data, and studies of human behavior, there is an opportunity to bring a grasp of the biological and ecological processes that drive transmission of malaria into the arena of malaria research and control programs.

Continuing worldwide efforts to eradicate malaria will require maintaining robust surveillance and applying adaptive control in areas where transmission seems to have declined, which may otherwise result in return.

The person–place–time framework offers a powerful way to understand malaria transmission dynamics and develop responsive control plans tailored to changing epidemiological situations.
